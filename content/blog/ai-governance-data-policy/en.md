If anyone on your team has asked ChatGPT to summarize a contract, draft a proposal or crunch a sales spreadsheet, AI is already inside your business. The question isn't whether to use it, but under what rules.

You're not alone. Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 Work Trend Index found that 78% of AI users bring their own AI tools to work (what the report calls BYOAI), and that share rises to 80% at small and medium-sized companies. Small businesses need guardrails for that, just lighter ones than a bank would.

## What is AI governance?

AI governance is the set of rules that decides which AI tools your company can use, with what data, who approves them and who is accountable when something goes wrong. You don't need a compliance department: for a small business it can be a two-page policy and one named owner.

AI ethics asks a different question: even if something is possible and legal, is it fair and transparent to your customers and employees? Screening resumes with AI saves time, but it can also reject candidates for reasons nobody ever reviewed.

## The 4 most common risks

- Data leaks: an employee pastes a customer list, payroll or health data into a free chatbot. Depending on the tool and its settings, that data may be stored or used for training.
- Made-up answers: generative AI can produce numbers, laws or citations that sound right and are wrong. If nobody checks, the error reaches your customer.
- Autonomous agents with too much access: an agent connected to email, banking or your CRM can take actions nobody approved. Give agents the minimum permissions and log what they do.
- Shadow AI: every department uses a different tool on personal accounts, and no one knows what data has left the company.

Ignoring these risks has a measurable cost. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report, conducted by the Ponemon Institute, found that organizations with high levels of shadow AI paid an average of $670,000 more per breach than those with low or no shadow AI. It also found that 63% of breached organizations either had no AI governance policy or were still developing one.

## A 5-step AI policy

1. Take inventory: ask each team which AI tools they use today (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, browser extensions, agents) and for what tasks.
2. Classify your data in three tiers: public (fine to use with AI), internal (approved tools only) and confidential (never in an external AI: SSNs, bank details, health records, credentials).
3. Approve tools: favor business plans that don't train on your data and let you manage accounts. Ban personal accounts for work.
4. Require human review: anything AI produces that goes to a customer, a bank or a regulator gets checked by a person. Agents get read-only access until proven safe.
5. Train and repeat: a one-hour session on AI dos and don'ts beats a document no one reads. Review the policy every six months.

## What about regulations?

The U.S. has no single federal AI law, but existing rules still apply to the data you feed AI: HIPAA for health information, state privacy laws such as California's CCPA, and sector rules in finance. If you sell to Europe, the GDPR and the EU AI Act may apply as well. A policy doesn't replace legal advice, but it gets your house in order before anyone asks.

## Start with your data, not the tool

The companies getting real value from AI aren't the ones with the most tools. They're the ones that know what data they have, where it lives and who can see it. That's what a data audit delivers: the groundwork that turns AI into an advantage instead of a liability.
