Most companies have tried AI, but few see the return in their numbers. In McKinsey's The state of AI in 2025 survey, 88% of organizations reported regular AI use in at least one business function, yet only 39% reported any enterprise-level EBIT impact (earnings before interest and taxes).

Gartner saw this coming in 2024, when it predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, due to poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs or unclear business value. It's rarely the technology that fails: many projects start with a tool instead of a business problem, and nobody measures whether they're worth the cost.

This guide is for owners and managers of small and mid-sized businesses who want to use generative AI where it actually earns its keep. There's room to get ahead: the U.S. Census Bureau found that 19.8% of U.S. businesses were using AI as of May 2026, and that 37% of firms with 250 or more employees were.

## What is generative AI?

Generative AI creates new content (text, images, code or audio) from instructions written in plain language. ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Microsoft Copilot are examples. It's different from traditional analytics or machine learning, which predict or classify from historical data, like forecasting next month's sales. Generative AI drafts; analytics measures.

That distinction matters for ROI: generative AI saves time on language-heavy work, but it doesn't know your numbers unless you connect it to clean, reliable data.

## Where it pays off for small businesses

- Customer support: drafting replies to common questions and summarizing tickets, with a person approving before anything goes out.
- Sales and marketing: first drafts of proposals, product descriptions, emails and social posts that your team edits.
- Back office: summarizing contracts, pulling data out of invoices and PDFs, and writing meeting notes and standard procedures.
- Internal knowledge: answering employee questions from your own manuals and policies, so people stop digging through folders.
- Reporting: turning dashboards and spreadsheets into plain-English summaries for managers.

## Where it usually doesn't

- Tasks where a mistake is expensive and hard to spot: legal advice, tax filings, or medical and financial decisions without expert review.
- Processes that are already broken. AI speeds up a bad process; it doesn't fix it.
- Anything that depends on data you don't trust. If your sales numbers live in five spreadsheets that disagree, AI will confidently summarize the wrong one.

## How to measure ROI in 5 steps

1. Pick one process and measure the baseline: hours per week, error rate and how long customers wait.
2. Define one success metric before you start, such as hours saved, response time or cost per ticket. "Use more AI" is not a metric.
3. Count the full cost: per-seat licenses, setup time, training, integrations and the hours people spend reviewing AI output.
4. Run a 30- to 60-day pilot with a small team and compare it against the baseline, not against expectations.
5. Decide with numbers: scale it, adjust it or shut it down. Canceling a pilot that didn't pay off is a good decision, not a failure.

A simple formula: ROI = (value of hours saved + added revenue − total cost) ÷ total cost. For example, if a $30-a-month tool saves an employee five hours a month and their time is worth $40 an hour, it delivers about $200 of value for $30. If it saves 30 minutes, it doesn't pay for itself.

## Start with your data, then the tool

The businesses that keep investing in AI are the ones that can prove it works. That proof comes from clean data and a dashboard that tracks the metrics you chose. Business intelligence is what turns an AI experiment into a decision you can defend.
