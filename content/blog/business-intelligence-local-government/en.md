A resident calls city hall to ask about a building permit they applied for five weeks ago. The clerk puts them on hold, walks over to the building department, and someone digs through a spreadsheet to find out the application is waiting on a fire review that nobody scheduled.

That call happens every day in thousands of small cities and counties. Not because staff don't care, but because the information lives in five different systems and nobody can see the whole picture.

Business intelligence fixes exactly that. It's usually associated with companies, but a city is also an organization with customers (residents), a budget, operations and service levels. In this article we explain what it means for local government, what the latest data says, and we apply it to a typical small city.

## What business intelligence means for a city

Business intelligence (BI) is the practice of pulling data from the systems you already use, cleaning it and turning it into dashboards that update themselves. If you want the basics first, start with our [practical guide to business intelligence for small business](/en/blog/business-intelligence-small-business/): the principles are the same.

In a city, the data is already there:

- The **permitting system** knows when every application came in and where it is.
- The **311 or work order system** records every pothole, streetlight and missed trash pickup.
- The **financial system** has the budget and every expense by department.
- The **utility billing system** knows how much water each account was charged for.
- **Payroll** knows how many overtime hours each department logs.

The problem is that each system answers questions about itself. Nobody can easily answer the questions that matter to a city manager or a council: where are we falling behind, where is money leaking, and which neighborhoods are being served worst.

## What the data says about local governments

Three recent sources help size the opportunity.

**There are a lot of local governments, and most are small.** The U.S. Census Bureau counted 91,438 local governments in 2025, up from 90,837 in 2022. That includes 3,031 counties, 19,489 municipal governments, 16,184 townships and 12,535 independent school districts. And most cities are small: in 2024, 75% of the country's 19,479 incorporated places, 14,603 cities and towns, had fewer than 5,000 residents. Only 4.2%, 817 cities, had 50,000 people or more.

**Their technology teams are stretched thin.** The 2026 County CIO Insights report from Deloitte, based on a survey of county IT leaders, found that nearly half of counties (48%) have 10 or fewer full-time staff supporting county IT. 53% dedicate less than 10% of their IT budget to innovation rather than maintenance, and two-thirds run environments that are at least 75% on premises. 45% cite budget limitations as a barrier to modernization, while only 14% point to a lack of executive support.

**They measure less than they do.** The same report found that 86% of counties offer training, but only 21% have program measurement and reporting in place. Among the strategies counties use to cut costs, 48% cite automation tools. And 34% already use generative AI, with another 23% planning to adopt it within 12 months.

Put together, the picture is clear. The intent to modernize is there, and leadership supports it. What's missing is capacity: small teams spending most of their time keeping systems running, with little left over to measure results. That's exactly the gap a well-scoped BI project fills, because it doesn't replace any system. It connects the ones you already have.

## The city in our example

Picture a city of 18,000 residents with 140 employees and an IT team of three people. It has a building department, public works, a water utility, police and a finance office that reports to the city council every month.

Its problems sound familiar:

- Residents complain that permits take forever, but nobody can say how long they actually take or where they get stuck.
- The 311 system logs requests, but nobody knows which neighborhoods wait the longest.
- The monthly budget report for the council takes the finance director almost a week to put together in Excel.
- The water utility produces more water than it bills, and nobody knows exactly how much or where it's going.
- Overtime in public works keeps going up, and the budget shows it only at the end of the year.

All five can be tackled with data the city already has.

## 5 dashboards for a small city

### 1. Permits and inspections: where applications get stuck

It's the dashboard residents notice the most, because a permit delay hits their project, their contractor and their money.

The dashboard shows every open application by type and stage: intake, plan review, fire review, inspection, approval. It shows how many days each one has been waiting and which stage is the bottleneck this week.

![Chart of the average number of days to issue a building permit over 12 months in a small city: between 32 and 36 days in the first five months, then a steady drop after the dashboard goes live in month 6, down to 19 days in month 12](/images/blog/business-intelligence-local-government/permit-days-to-issue.svg "The dashboard doesn't issue permits: it shows where they're waiting, so the team can act on it.")

In the city of our example, the first surprise was that the problem wasn't plan review: it was fire review, which had a single inspector and no schedule. Once that was visible, the city set aside two fixed days a week for it. In six months, the average time to issue a permit dropped from 34 to 19 days, and the backlog went from 210 to 85 open applications.

### 2. 311 requests: who waits the longest

Every pothole, broken streetlight and missed pickup is a small promise to a resident. The dashboard shows open requests on a map, the median time to close each type, and the neighborhoods where requests pile up.

The city found that requests from two neighborhoods took twice as long to close, not because of neglect, but because the work order system routed them to a crew that covered a much larger area. After redrawing the zones, the median time to close a request dropped from 9 to 4 days.

### 3. Budget vs. actual: no surprises in December

The finance dashboard compares, every month, what each department has spent against its budget, and projects where it will end the fiscal year if it keeps spending at the same pace.

Before, the finance director spent about 30 hours putting together the monthly council report by copying figures from the financial system into Excel. With a dashboard that refreshes automatically, the report takes 4 hours, and those are spent on analysis, not on copying and pasting. If your city still lives in spreadsheets, our guide to [automating an Excel dashboard with Power BI](/en/blog/excel-dashboard-auto-refresh-power-bi/) shows the first step.

### 4. Water billing: the water you produce but never bill

Every water utility loses some water to leaks, faulty meters and billing errors. The dashboard compares the water produced with the water billed, by sector and by month, and flags accounts whose consumption suddenly drops to zero or stays exactly the same for months: a typical sign of a broken meter.

In the city of our example, unbilled water stood at 18%. The dashboard pointed to 300 accounts with frozen meters and two sectors with losses far above the rest, where crews found two major leaks. A year later, unbilled water was down to 13%, which means more revenue without raising rates.

### 5. Overtime: where the hours go

Overtime is one of the easiest costs to lose control of. The dashboard shows overtime hours by department, crew and reason, every two weeks, not at the end of the year.

Public works discovered that most of its overtime came from emergency callouts that could have been scheduled work: preventive maintenance that kept getting postponed. By reorganizing the schedule, it cut overtime by 12% in six months.

The numbers in this city are illustrative: yours will be different. What repeats is the pattern. None of these dashboards required a new system or new data. The information was already in the permitting, 311, financial, billing and payroll systems.

## How to start without a big project

A city doesn't need a multi-year transformation program to start using business intelligence. It needs a first visible win.

1. **Pick one question the council asks over and over.** "How long do permits take?" is a better starting point than "we want to be data-driven."
2. **Find out where the data lives.** Usually it's one or two systems and a few spreadsheets. Check whether it can be exported or connected directly.
3. **Agree on definitions.** When does a permit "start": when the application is submitted or when it's complete? If the departments don't agree, the dashboard will cause arguments instead of decisions.
4. **Start with a small pilot.** One dashboard, one department, 30 to 60 days. A scope that size often fits within your procurement rules for small purchases, which vary by state and city.
5. **Use the tools you already pay for.** Many governments already license Microsoft tools, and Power BI often fits there. Compare options in our guide to [BI tools for small business](/en/blog/business-intelligence-tools-small-business/).
6. **Assign an owner.** Every dashboard needs someone who reviews it every week and acts on it.

## Common mistakes

- **Building a dashboard for residents before one for staff.** Public transparency dashboards are valuable, but if the internal team doesn't use the data first, the public one ends up showing figures nobody trusts.
- **Ignoring data quality.** If the same street appears under four different spellings in the 311 system, the map will lie. Our guide to [data governance for small business](/en/blog/data-governance-small-business/) applies to cities too.
- **Exposing personal data.** Residents' names, addresses and account numbers must not appear in shared dashboards. Aggregate by area, control who sees what, and check your state's public records rules.
- **Measuring everything at once.** Twenty indicators on one screen are the same as none. Start with three to five that answer a real question.
- **Treating it as an IT project.** BI succeeds when the department that owns the problem owns the dashboard. IT connects the data; the building official, the finance director or the public works manager decide what to do with it.
